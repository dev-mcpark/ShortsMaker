# Source-grounded Shorts format

This is the editorial recipe for the next review cut, not a claim that it already performs well. [STATUS.md](STATUS.md) owns the current experiment and results; [QUALITY_EVALUATION.md](QUALITY_EVALUATION.md) owns how to score them.

## Build the story before buying visuals

1. Before drafting scenes, name the **concrete consequence for a nonspecialist**, the source-backed cause or decision to reveal, and the payoff. If the consequence is only that a mechanism works differently, keep searching. The NASA, CW-Net, and wind-tunnel cuts passed factual/technical checks but did not interest the owner on 2026-10-09; a clever niche mechanism is not sufficient evidence of appeal. The owner watches architecture, economics, and everyday-life topics among many others, so explore across subject areas rather than treating one genre or one owner's reaction as an audience metric. A source preview needs only a one-line question and proof link before a full script or local cut. Keep the primary article URL and extract its **question, observed decision, evidence, and limit**. Separate a device's capability or assigned role from an action actually observed in the test; a source saying a rover uses an arm for sampling does not by itself prove that a sample was collected in this run. Likewise, label a simulation or model projection as such rather than narrating it as a measured field result. A specific first-second question is a working hypothesis drawn from [YouTube's Shorts deep dive](https://blog.youtube/creator-and-artist-stories/youtube-shorts-deep-dive/) and [Korean creator workshop](https://blog.youtube/intl/ko-kr/creator-and-artist-stories/shorts-workshop-kr-2025/); do not add a dilemma absent from the source or show its answer in the opening graphics before the story turn.
2. Reveal the actual decision early, then give each following scene a new causal step or piece of evidence rather than restating the result. The approved five-scene Sillim draft asks one concrete "why" question, explains the two pressures and the physical layout, then attributes the expected water-level effect to Seoul City; use its direct spoken pace as a reference, not a fixed topic template. For the [NASA field test](https://science.nasa.gov/blogs/planetary-expeditions/2026/09/15/nasa-field-tests-ai-fleet-capability-for-science-exploration/), the lead drone briefly paused to process a new point of interest but did **not** abandon its original task; another robotic asset investigated the new question. Do not turn that pause into "the mission never stopped" or identify the second asset more narrowly than the source. End with the limit that this was a Virginia Earth test, not mission footage from another world.
3. Map each sentence to one visual proof: the credited source photo for what was photographed, or an explicitly labeled diagram for the choice, branch, or changing state. Reveal diagram elements as the narration reaches them. On a source photo, point to confirmed objects in sequence while keeping the credit visible; do not cover the object being identified. A camera move alone is not explanatory motion. Do not imply invented AI scenery is documentary footage.
4. Listen to the story before building visuals: on macOS, `scripts/prepare_scratch_narration.py` produces script-bound Yuna scenes and an audio-only review page under ignored `outputs/`, rejecting unusable audio and reusing verified files. If the story is worth developing, make a no-API storyboard with local motion. Reveal captions and diagram conclusions as their phrases are spoken; compare local narration pauses, caption boundaries, and sampled final frames because character-based timing is only an estimate. Check the first five seconds, subtitle safe area, factual labels, immediate scene cuts, static stretches, each scene's distinct composition, and the complete sound mix. A frame-difference count can locate frozen stretches but cannot judge whether the motion helps the story. One human rejection is cheaper than another paid render.
5. Before selecting music, write one sentence in `music_direction` describing the story's emotional arc, strength of the beat under speech, and a mood to avoid. It is an editorial brief, not an automatic match or a license check. Only after the story and visual beats work, record one final narration set and reuse it. Add music with a recorded source and no-attribution license; choose the track intentionally against that brief and check that speech stays intelligible. The editor checks a neighboring `.license.json` file and matching MP3 hash before paid media work. For local comparisons, use `scripts/local_review.py --bgm-track` with an exact documented MP3 filename; Studio has an exact **BGM Track** choice for the same decision. A broad mood is useful for drafts, but does not establish fit; an out-of-list mood from automatic planning now stops for review instead of silently becoming `energetic`. The owner rejected “Feedback Dreams” for the NASA story, then preferred documented Mixkit “Digital Clouds” over “Stylz” and `soft` captions over `card` in the controlled A cuts on 2026-10-04. The selected `futuristic_digital_clouds.mp3` and `soft` captions remain the NASA reference. New edits normalize the music source near -14 LUFS before applying the 20% mix; this may sound slightly different from the already-reviewed cut, so compare locally before replacing it. This is a pilot preference, not a universal mood mapping or full-video approval. Other legacy `bgm/*.mp3` files remain unverified. Mix levels are starting points, not universal loudness guarantees.

## Budget and acceptance

The default for this milestone is **$0 in new paid calls** until the owner sets a round limit. `PAID_PRODUCTION_ENABLED=false` now enforces this for `ProductionService`, `scripts/paired_review.py`, and direct OpenAI/Google/Brave request helpers; complete local media still render. Re-enable only after choosing a round limit and checking provider usage. The switch is not a spending cap. Reuse local source material, audio, and cached clips. `MAX_VISUAL_COST_USD` only estimates **new visual output**, including an explicit reserve for multiple Veo attempts; it is not a provider billing cap. The service subtracts cached/manual scenes; direct `VideoGenerator` calls check the full requested batch or one scene without that cache credit, and the standalone character-video helper checks its single silent clip. Other direct helpers have no monetary preflight. Record OpenAI, Google input/output, failed calls, provider-side retries, taxes, and any music license separately before comparing against the round cap. Google Cloud's ₩10,000 monthly email budget is alerts-only and does not cap either provider's spending. Check current rates just before a paid run.

For scale, the published rates checked on 2026-10-02 were about **$0.004** for 260 characters of [OpenAI `tts-1`](https://developers.openai.com/api/docs/models/tts-1), **$0.034 plus input tokens** for one 1K Gemini 3.1 Flash-Lite image, and **$0.48** for six seconds of Veo 3.1 Fast video-only 720p ([Google Cloud pricing](https://cloud.google.com/gemini-enterprise-agent-platform/generative-ai/pricing)). These are component estimates, not a run total or a billing guarantee.

Do not buy a generated visual merely to create movement. Buy one only when it communicates a source-grounded moment better than a photo or diagram and its estimated worst case fits the round limit. The first accepted outcome is an owner-approved private review cut that passes technical and factual checks. Public publication comes later. [YouTube's monetization policy](https://support.google.com/youtube/answer/1311392?hl=en-gb) flags repetitive mass-produced slideshows with little original narrative value, so the production target is an explanation with a distinct story and meaningful visual evidence.

For published videos, compare first-second hold or viewed-versus-swiped where available, average view duration or retention, factual/editorial acceptance, and billed cost per accepted video over the same observation window. [YouTube's Shorts analytics guide](https://support.google.com/youtube/answer/12942217?co=YOUTUBE._YTVideoType%3Dshorts&hl=en) explains the viewed-versus-swiped metric. Treat early numbers as observations, not proof from one video.
