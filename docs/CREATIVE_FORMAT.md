# Source-grounded Shorts format

This is the editorial recipe for the next review cut, not a claim that it already performs well. [STATUS.md](STATUS.md) owns the current experiment and results; [QUALITY_EVALUATION.md](QUALITY_EVALUATION.md) owns how to score them.

## Build the story before buying visuals

1. Keep the primary article URL and extract its **question, observed decision, evidence, and limit**. Separate a device's capability or assigned role from an action actually observed in the test; a source saying a rover uses an arm for sampling does not by itself prove that a sample was collected in this run. A specific first-second question is a working hypothesis drawn from [YouTube's Shorts deep dive](https://blog.youtube/creator-and-artist-stories/youtube-shorts-deep-dive/) and [Korean creator workshop](https://blog.youtube/intl/ko-kr/creator-and-artist-stories/shorts-workshop-kr-2025/); do not add a dilemma absent from the source or show its answer in the opening graphics before the story turn.
2. Reveal the actual decision early, then give each following scene a new causal step or piece of evidence rather than restating the result. For the [NASA field test](https://science.nasa.gov/blogs/planetary-expeditions/2026/09/15/nasa-field-tests-ai-fleet-capability-for-science-exploration/), the lead drone briefly paused to process a new point of interest but did **not** abandon its original task; another robotic asset investigated the new question. Do not turn that pause into "the mission never stopped" or identify the second asset more narrowly than the source. End with the limit that this was a Virginia Earth test, not mission footage from another world.
3. Map each sentence to one visual proof: the credited source photo for what was photographed, or an explicitly labeled diagram for the choice, branch, or changing state. Reveal diagram elements as the narration reaches them. On a source photo, point to confirmed objects in sequence while keeping the credit visible; do not cover the object being identified. A camera move alone is not explanatory motion. Do not imply invented AI scenery is documentary footage.
4. Make a no-API storyboard with local speech and motion first. Reveal captions and diagram conclusions as their phrases are spoken; compare local narration pauses, caption boundaries, and sampled final frames because character-based timing is only an estimate. Check the first five seconds, subtitle safe area, factual labels, immediate scene cuts, static stretches, each scene's distinct composition, and the complete sound mix. A frame-difference count can locate frozen stretches but cannot judge whether the motion helps the story. One human rejection is cheaper than another paid render.
5. Only after the story and visual beats work, record one final narration set and reuse it. Add music with a recorded source and no-attribution license; choose the track intentionally and check that speech stays intelligible. The editor checks a neighboring `.license.json` file and matching MP3 hash before paid media work. For local comparisons, use `scripts/local_review.py --bgm-track` with an exact documented MP3 filename so a broad mood cannot silently choose another song. The owner rejected “Feedback Dreams” for the NASA story. Compare documented Mixkit “Digital Clouds” at 20% with “Stylz” at 12% using otherwise identical A cuts; neither has been accepted by ear. The default `sci-fi` track remains Digital Clouds, while Feedback Dreams remains documented for `mysterious`. Other legacy `bgm/*.mp3` files remain unverified. Mix levels are starting points, not universal loudness guarantees. The caption style now uses soft white text, a dark blue outline, and a slightly smaller Korean font; judge its feel in playback, not only still frames.

## Budget and acceptance

The default for this milestone is **$0 in new paid calls** until the owner sets a round limit. Reuse local source material, audio, and cached clips. `MAX_VISUAL_COST_USD` only estimates **new visual output**, including an explicit reserve for multiple Veo attempts; it is not a provider billing cap. Record OpenAI, Google input/output, failed calls, provider-side retries, taxes, and any music license separately before comparing against the round cap. Google Cloud's ₩10,000 monthly email budget is alerts-only and does not cap either provider's spending. Check current rates just before a paid run.

For scale, the published rates checked on 2026-10-02 were about **$0.004** for 260 characters of [OpenAI `tts-1`](https://developers.openai.com/api/docs/models/tts-1), **$0.034 plus input tokens** for one 1K Gemini 3.1 Flash-Lite image, and **$0.48** for six seconds of Veo 3.1 Fast video-only 720p ([Google Cloud pricing](https://cloud.google.com/gemini-enterprise-agent-platform/generative-ai/pricing)). These are component estimates, not a run total or a billing guarantee.

Do not buy a generated visual merely to create movement. Buy one only when it communicates a source-grounded moment better than a photo or diagram and its estimated worst case fits the round limit. The first accepted outcome is an owner-approved private review cut that passes technical and factual checks. Public publication comes later. [YouTube's monetization policy](https://support.google.com/youtube/answer/1311392?hl=en-gb) flags repetitive mass-produced slideshows with little original narrative value, so the production target is an explanation with a distinct story and meaningful visual evidence.

For published videos, compare first-second hold or viewed-versus-swiped where available, average view duration or retention, factual/editorial acceptance, and billed cost per accepted video over the same observation window. [YouTube's Shorts analytics guide](https://support.google.com/youtube/answer/12942217?co=YOUTUBE._YTVideoType%3Dshorts&hl=en) explains the viewed-versus-swiped metric. Treat early numbers as observations, not proof from one video.
